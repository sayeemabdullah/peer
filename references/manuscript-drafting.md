# Manuscript Drafting

Helps structure and clarify manuscript sections. This is drafting
assistance, not ghostwriting — the paper and its argument remain the
researcher's (Standing Rule 9).

## Before drafting anything

Read what the user already has — notes, an outline, prior drafts, the
underlying results — before generating prose. Ask what stage this section is
at: a blank page, a rough draft needing structure, or a polished draft
needing line-level tightening. These call for different kinds of help.

## Structure and clarity per section

- **Introduction**: motivates the question, situates it in the literature
  (cross-reference `literature-review.md` for citation integrity), and
  states the hypothesis/aim clearly without giving away results prematurely.
  Check that the gap being closed is stated precisely, not just gestured at.
- **Methods**: enough detail for replication — check against
  `reporting-standards.md` for the field's expected checklist. Flag missing
  detail (exact measures, exclusion criteria, analysis software/version)
  rather than smoothing over the gap with vaguer language.
- **Results**: report what was found, including null/failed
  results (Standing Rule 6), with effect sizes and intervals alongside any
  significance tests (Standing Rule 3). Results sections should describe,
  not argue — save interpretation for discussion.
- **Discussion**: connects results back to the hypothesis, addresses
  limitations honestly, and scopes claims to what the design actually
  supports. Watch specifically for the conclusion drifting broader than the
  results section warrants — this is the most common place overclaiming
  creeps in, and exactly what `adversarial-review.md` will target later.
- **Abstract**: written or revised last, and checked word-for-word against
  what the results section actually shows — abstracts are where reporting
  standards most often quietly slip under word-count pressure.

## Never silently rewrite

If asked to "clean up" or "improve" a section, clarify what that means
before making sweeping changes: tightening sentences and fixing structure is
different from changing the argument or softening/strengthening a claim. If
a requested change would alter what's being claimed (not just how it's
phrased), flag that distinction explicitly rather than making the change
silently — that's a substantive edit, not a style pass, and the user should
decide it consciously.

If the user hands over a full draft and says "make this sound better"
without further direction, don't take that as license to rewrite it in a
different voice. Preserve their sentence-level style and word choices where
possible; suggest structural and clarity fixes rather than replacing prose
wholesale. If a full rewrite genuinely seems like the better path, say so
and ask before doing it.

## Working alongside adversarial-review

Drafting and critique are separate acts. If a draft has a substantive
problem (a claim not supported by the results, a missing effect size), name
it — don't just quietly write around it in a way that hides the underlying
issue. If the user wants a full critique rather than drafting help, route to
`adversarial-review.md` instead.
