# Field Layer: Qualitative Research

Covers interview studies, ethnography, focus groups, case studies, and
qualitative analysis across disciplines. **Most of Peer's default machinery
assumes quantitative research and does not apply here.** Applying
quantitative standards to qualitative work is a category error, and one that
reviewers from quantitative backgrounds make constantly.

## What does not apply

- **Sample size calculations and power analysis.** The logic is different:
  qualitative sampling is purposive, aimed at richness and range, not at
  estimating a population parameter.
- **Effect sizes, p-values, generalizability to a population.** Qualitative
  work aims at transferability — enough contextual detail that readers can
  judge applicability to their setting — not statistical generalization.
- **Inter-rater reliability as a universal requirement.** It suits content
  analysis with a fixed codebook; it is inappropriate for interpretive
  approaches where multiple readings are the point rather than a problem.
  Demanding a kappa from a reflexive thematic analysis misunderstands the
  method.
- **"Saturation" as a checkbox.** It's frequently invoked without meaning.
  If claimed, say what kind (code saturation vs. meaning saturation), how it
  was assessed, and against what stopping criterion — an unexamined
  saturation claim is weaker than an honest statement of what constrained
  the sample.

## What rigor actually looks like

- **Methodological coherence**: the research question, theoretical framework,
  sampling, data collection, and analysis method should align. A study
  claiming grounded theory while doing thematic summary, or claiming
  phenomenology while reporting frequency counts, is incoherent — this is
  the most common and most substantive qualitative critique.
- **Name the specific approach**: reflexive thematic analysis, grounded
  theory (and which variant — Glaserian, Straussian, constructivist),
  interpretative phenomenological analysis, discourse analysis, framework
  analysis. "Themes emerged" is not a method; themes are constructed by an
  analyst, and saying otherwise obscures the analytic work.
- **Reflexivity**: the researcher's position, background, and relationship to
  participants shape what was collected and how it was read. A reflexivity
  statement should be specific about how position affected the work, not a
  ritual disclosure.
- **Analytic transparency**: how coding proceeded, how codes became themes,
  whether an audit trail exists, how disconfirming cases were handled.
  Actively seeking negative cases is a strong rigor marker.
- **Evidence for claims**: quotes should support interpretations, with enough
  context to judge, and drawn from across participants rather than
  repeatedly from one articulate person. Report how many participants
  contributed to each theme.
- **Member checking** where appropriate to the approach — though note it
  isn't universally suitable, and returning interpretations to participants
  raises its own questions in critical or psychoanalytic traditions.

## Sampling

- Purposive, theoretical, maximum-variation, or snowball — name it and
  justify it against the question.
- Describe participants well enough for readers to judge transferability,
  within the limits of confidentiality.
- Report recruitment, refusals, and who is absent from the sample. Who
  declined shapes findings as much as who participated.

## Reporting standards

- **COREQ** for interview and focus group studies (32 items).
- **SRQR** as a more flexible alternative across qualitative designs.
- **ENTREQ** for syntheses of qualitative research.
- Journals increasingly require one; check which the target expects. Note
  that checklist compliance is not the same as rigor — a COREQ-complete
  paper can still be methodologically incoherent.

## Ethics

- Qualitative data is often deeply identifying: small communities,
  distinctive roles, verbatim speech patterns. Standard anonymization is
  frequently insufficient — consider composite descriptions, and be explicit
  about the tradeoff with analytic transparency.
- Sensitive topics require distress protocols and a plan for disclosures
  requiring action.
- Consent is ongoing, not a one-time signature, particularly in ethnographic
  work where the relationship evolves.
- Data sharing is genuinely constrained here — raw transcripts often cannot
  be shared responsibly. Say so rather than treating non-sharing as a
  failing.

## Mixed methods

If the study combines strands, state the design and how integration
happened. A qualitative section appended to a quantitative study without
integration isn't mixed methods (`nursing-health-services.md`).

## Publication norms

- Journals vary enormously by discipline; qualitative work is
  under-represented in some quantitative-dominated venues, which is a
  submission-strategy consideration (`submission-strategy.md`) rather than a
  reflection of quality.
- Word limits are a real constraint — qualitative papers need space for
  data extracts, and journals with short limits may not be viable.

## Red flags

- Method named as one approach but executed as another
- "Themes emerged from the data" with no analytic process described
- Quotes drawn overwhelmingly from one or two participants
- Saturation asserted with no criterion
- Percentages and frequency counts used to imply representativeness
