# Grant Writing

Covers proposals and specific aims pages — funding the study before Phase 2
begins. Distinct from `manuscript-drafting.md`: a grant argues that work
*should* be funded and *can* be done; a manuscript reports work already
done.

## Specific aims

The specific aims page is usually the highest-leverage page in the whole
proposal — often the only page every reviewer reads closely. Push for:
- A clear statement of the problem and why it matters, in the first
  paragraph, before any background detail
- A gap in current knowledge or capability, stated precisely enough that the
  proposed work visibly fills it
- Aims that are individually testable/achievable and collectively add up to
  the stated goal — flag aims that are too dependent on each other (if Aim 1
  fails, does the whole proposal collapse?) or too disconnected (do the aims
  actually build toward one goal, or read as three separate projects?)
- A closing paragraph stating the expected outcome and its significance —
  what changes if this works

## Significance and innovation framing

- **Significance**: why does this matter to the field, and ideally beyond
  it? Push past generic claims ("this is an understudied area") toward the
  concrete consequence of the knowledge gap persisting.
- **Innovation**: what's actually new here — conceptual framework, method,
  population, technology, or a novel combination of existing ones? Don't let
  "innovation" sections overclaim; reviewers who know the field will notice
  if a described innovation is actually standard practice elsewhere.
- Load-bearing citations here follow the same standing rule as everywhere
  else (Rule 5, Rule 8) — a significance argument built on a single study or
  an unverifiable claim is fragile under review.

## Budget justification

- Every line item should map to a specific aim or task — reviewers and
  program officers check for this alignment. Flag budget items that don't
  obviously connect to the described work.
- Personnel effort (person-months/FTE) should be realistic against the
  described scope of work; padded or implausibly low effort both draw
  scrutiny.
- If cost-sharing, subcontracts, or equipment are involved, make sure the
  justification for each is concrete, not boilerplate.

## Preliminary data

Distinguish clearly between preliminary data that demonstrates feasibility
(the team can do the proposed work) and preliminary data that's being used
to sneak in a confirmatory claim the actual study hasn't tested yet. If
preliminary results are described with confirmatory-sounding language,
flag it — reviewers read this as a sign the "hypothesis" may already be a
foregone conclusion, which undercuts rather than strengthens the case for
funding new work (this connects to Standing Rule 2 on HARKing).

## Resubmission strategy

For a proposal that was previously scored but not funded:
- Read the summary statement/reviewer critiques closely before drafting the
  introduction to resubmission — it should address the specific concerns
  raised, not generically strengthen the proposal
- A short, direct "introduction to resubmission" page (where the mechanism
  allows one) should map concern → change made, similar in spirit to
  `response-to-reviewers.md` — reuse that sub-skill's point-by-point
  discipline here
- Flag if a prior critique is being ignored rather than addressed; reviewers
  on resubmission specifically check whether prior concerns were taken
  seriously

## What this sub-skill does not do

It doesn't guess at funder-specific formatting rules or page limits it
hasn't been told — ask for the specific mechanism/funder (NIH R01, NSF
CAREER, foundation-specific RFP, etc.) since requirements vary, and don't
assume one funder's conventions apply to another's.
