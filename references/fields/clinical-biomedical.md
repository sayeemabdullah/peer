# Field Layer: Clinical / Biomedical

## Reporting standards

- **CONSORT** for randomized controlled trials, including the extensions
  for specific trial types (cluster, non-inferiority, pragmatic, pilot).
- **STROBE** for observational studies (cohort, case-control,
  cross-sectional).
- **PRISMA** for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
- **ARRIVE** for animal research.
- **TRIPOD** for diagnostic/prognostic prediction models.
- Trial registration (ClinicalTrials.gov or an equivalent WHO-recognized
  registry) is typically mandatory *before* enrollment for interventional
  trials, not optional best practice — many journals (following ICMJE
  policy) will not publish an unregistered trial. This directly
  operationalizes Standing Rule 1 in this field: the registration itself is
  the locked plan.

## Adequate sample size

- Sample size is normally justified against a clinically meaningful effect
  size (e.g., a minimum clinically important difference), not just a
  statistically detectable one — a trial can be adequately powered
  statistically while being powered to detect an effect too small to matter
  clinically. Push for the clinical significance threshold specifically
  when reviewing a power analysis in this field.
- For trials, the sample size calculation and any interim analysis/stopping
  rules should be specified in the protocol before enrollment, and any
  deviation (e.g., stopping early for efficacy or futility) should be
  reported with the correction it required (`power-analysis.md`,
  `avoiding-p-hacking.md`).

## Ethics/IRB specifics

- Human subjects review is mandatory and typically more stringent given
  direct health risk — IRB or equivalent (e.g., an NHS Research Ethics
  Committee), often alongside a Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) for
  trials with meaningful risk.
- Informed consent requirements are typically strict and well-defined;
  waivers need specific, documented justification (e.g., emergency research
  exceptions).
- Vulnerable populations especially relevant here: pediatric patients,
  pregnant individuals, cognitively impaired or incapacitated patients,
  individuals in emergency/critical care situations who may not be able to
  give standard informed consent.
- Conflicts of interest (industry funding, consulting relationships,
  patents) require explicit disclosure and are scrutinized closely in this
  field.

## Publication norms

- Preprint posting (medRxiv, bioRxiv) is accepted at a growing number of
  venues but some clinical journals still restrict or have specific
  policies (e.g., requiring disclosure, or discouraging preprints of
  findings with direct clinical/public-health implications before peer
  review) — check the target journal specifically before assuming
  compatibility.
- ICMJE authorship criteria are the field-standard reference for who
  qualifies as an author versus warrants acknowledgment only.
- Data sharing statements are typically required at submission (even if the
  underlying data itself isn't always shared, due to patient privacy).

## Red flags

- A trial reported without a registration number, or with results that
  don't match what was registered (changed primary outcome, added outcomes)
- Sample size justified by a clinically meaningful effect that appears only
  after results were already known
- Surrogate endpoints presented as if equivalent to the clinical outcome
  they're meant to proxy, without acknowledging the gap
- A single small trial's effect size cited as if it were an established
  treatment effect, without noting it needs replication or a larger trial
  (Standing Rule 5, Standing Rule 7)
