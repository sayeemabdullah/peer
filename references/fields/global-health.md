# Field Layer: Global Health

Covers health research in low- and middle-income settings, international
health programs, and cross-national health work. Methodologically it draws
on `epidemiology-public-health.md` and `nursing-health-services.md`; what's
distinctive is that **the ethics and equity of the research relationship
are themselves methodological concerns**, not a separate compliance step.

## Equitable partnership

- **Parachute (helicopter) research** — researchers from high-income
  institutions collecting data in LMICs without meaningful local
  partnership — is both an ethical failure and increasingly a reason for
  desk rejection. Journals now ask about it directly.
- Check authorship: are researchers from the study country included, and in
  positions reflecting real contribution rather than courtesy? A study
  conducted entirely in one country with no authors from it invites the
  question, and the answer should appear in the paper.
- Local investigators should be involved in question framing, not only data
  collection — a study designed abroad and executed locally reproduces the
  problem even with local co-authors.
- Report capacity-strengthening components and how data and samples will be
  used afterward, including whether local partners retain access.
- Ethics approval is needed from **both** the local IRB and the sponsoring
  institution; report both.

## Consent and vulnerability

- Literacy, language, and power differentials affect meaningful consent.
  Report the consent process: language used, translation and
  back-translation, whether written or witnessed oral consent was used, and
  who obtained it.
- Where participants have limited access to healthcare, the offer of care
  through a study is a powerful inducement. Distinguish research from care
  clearly, and address therapeutic misconception explicitly.
- Community consent or engagement (community advisory boards, leader
  consultation) may be expected alongside individual consent, but does not
  replace it.
- **Standard of care in the control arm** is a long-standing contested
  issue: the comparator should generally be the best proven intervention,
  not the local status quo, and any deviation needs explicit justification.
- Post-trial access to a proven beneficial intervention should be addressed
  in advance.

## Measurement across contexts

- Instruments validated in one culture may not measure the same construct in
  another. Report translation, cultural adaptation, and local validation —
  and test measurement invariance before comparing scores across countries.
- Age, dates, and recall may be uncertain where documentation is limited;
  state how they were established.
- Verbal autopsy for cause of death, and other proxy measures used where
  vital registration is incomplete, carry substantial misclassification —
  report the method and its validation.
- Routine health system data quality varies; completeness and reporting
  rates should be characterized, not assumed.

## Study designs common here

- **Cluster randomized trials** are the norm for community interventions —
  see `nursing-health-services.md` for ICC and analysis requirements.
- **Stepped-wedge designs** are used where withholding an intervention is
  unacceptable; secular trend is confounded with rollout by design and must
  be modeled.
- Effectiveness in a controlled trial and effectiveness under program
  conditions differ; implementation and delivery constraints (supply chains,
  staffing, retention) determine real-world impact and belong in the
  discussion.
- Cost-effectiveness in LMIC settings uses different thresholds and often
  DALYs rather than QALYs (`health-economics.md`).

## Generalizability and framing

- Findings from one country or region should not be generalized to "LMICs"
  or to a continent. Name the setting specifically.
- Avoid framing that treats high-income practice as the default and other
  settings as deviations. Describe context as context.
- Report the health system context concretely enough for readers elsewhere
  to judge transferability.

## Data sharing

- Data sharing expectations must be balanced against local institutions'
  rights to their data and against re-identification risk in small
  populations. Agree governance in advance rather than deferring it to
  publication.
- Sample export requires material transfer agreements and, for genetic
  resources, Nagoya Protocol compliance.

## Publication norms

- Journals: Lancet Global Health, BMJ Global Health, PLOS Global Public
  Health, Global Health: Science and Practice, Bulletin of the WHO.
- Several now require statements on local researcher involvement and ethics
  approval location.
- Trial registration expected, including in regional registries (PACTR,
  CTRI) where applicable.
- medRxiv preprints accepted.

## Red flags specific to this field

- No authors from the country where data was collected
- Ethics approval only from the high-income sponsoring institution
- A control arm receiving less than the best proven intervention, without
  justification
- Instruments used across cultures with no adaptation or invariance testing
- Findings from one setting generalized to a region or income group
