# Giving Peer Review

Covers reviewing someone else's manuscript — structured, constructive,
specific critique that separates fatal flaws from fixable ones. Distinct
from `adversarial-review.md`: that sub-skill is for the user's own paper and
is deliberately unsparing; this one is for reviewing a colleague's or
stranger's work under a journal's or conference's review process, where the
goal is a genuinely useful review, not just finding every flaw.

## Read the actual manuscript

Same discipline as adversarial review: read the actual submitted document,
not an abstract-level skim. A review based on a partial read is easy for an
editor or the authors to spot and undermines the reviewer's credibility.

## Structure the review

1. **Brief summary** of what the paper claims to do and find, in the
   reviewer's own words — this shows the authors and editor the reviewer
   understood the paper correctly, and surfaces misunderstandings early if
   the authors' response disagrees with the summary.
2. **Overall assessment** — is the core question worth answering, is the
   approach sound, does the evidence support the conclusion. State this
   directly rather than burying it in a list of minor comments.
3. **Major comments** — issues that affect whether the conclusions are
   supported: methodological flaws, missing controls, insufficient
   evidence for the claims, a mismatch between the data and the analysis
   (cross-reference the same checks used in `adversarial-review.md`: HARKing,
   missing effect sizes, post-hoc exclusions, overclaiming, thin citation
   support).
4. **Minor comments** — clarity issues, missing details, presentation
   problems that don't threaten the paper's validity.
5. Keep major and minor separated, never interleaved — an editor and the
   authors need to know quickly what's fatal-adjacent versus polish.

## Constructive, not just critical

- Every major criticism should be actionable where possible: what
  additional analysis, data, or clarification would address it, not just
  "this is a problem." A good review helps the authors understand what
  would change the assessment.
- If something in the paper is genuinely well done, say so specifically —
  not as padding, but because it tells the authors what to keep as they
  revise, and because a review that reads as uniformly negative is less
  credible to an editor weighing it against other reviews.
- Avoid comments about the authors themselves (competence, effort,
  writing ability as a personal trait) — critique the work, not the people.

## Honesty over encouragement, still applies

Being constructive doesn't mean softening a fatal problem. If the central
claim isn't supported by the evidence, say so clearly in the overall
assessment, not just buried as one bullet among many minor ones (Standing
Rule 10).

## Recommendation

If the venue asks for a recommendation (accept / minor revision / major
revision / reject), make sure it's consistent with the body of the review —
a review full of major, unresolved concerns paired with a "minor revision"
recommendation is a common inconsistency editors have to chase down; flag
this mismatch if the user's draft recommendation doesn't match their own
listed comments.

## Confidentiality

Reviewed manuscripts are typically confidential pre-publication material.
Don't use content from a manuscript under review for anything beyond
drafting this review, and don't let details from it leak into unrelated
conversation context or outputs.
