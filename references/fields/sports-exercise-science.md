# Field Layer: Sports & Exercise Science

Covers exercise physiology, sports performance, biomechanics, and training
interventions. The structural constraint: **elite athlete populations are
tiny**, and the field's methods have adapted to small N in ways that are
sometimes legitimate and sometimes not.

## Small samples and the inference problem

- Studies with 8–20 participants are the norm. That is not automatically a
  flaw, but it makes underpowered null-hypothesis testing a poor tool, and
  it inflates published effect sizes through the winner's curse.
- **Magnitude-based inference (MBI)** was widely used in this field and has
  been shown to have inflated error rates; it is no longer defensible as a
  primary inferential method. If a paper uses it, say so — reviewers now
  reject it. Prefer estimation with confidence intervals, or Bayesian
  methods stated as such.
- Report effect sizes with intervals and interpret them against
  field-appropriate benchmarks, along with the smallest worthwhile change
  for the outcome, ideally derived from measurement error rather than a
  generic convention (`effect-size-over-significance.md`).
- Individual response variation is frequently over-interpreted: apparent
  "responders" and "non-responders" can arise entirely from measurement
  error and regression to the mean. Distinguishing true individual response
  requires a repeated-crossover design, not a single pre-post comparison.

## Measurement reliability is prerequisite

- Report the **typical error / coefficient of variation** for each
  performance measure in your lab and population. A performance change
  smaller than the measurement's typical error is not detectable.
- Familiarization trials matter — learning effects on performance tests are
  substantial and can masquerade as training adaptation.
- Standardize and report the conditions: time of day, prior training,
  nutritional state, hydration, ambient temperature, and equipment
  calibration. Performance is sensitive to all of them.
- For laboratory measures (VO₂max, lactate threshold, force plates), state
  the protocol and criteria — thresholds especially are definition-dependent
  and non-comparable across definitions.

## Training studies

- Report the full training prescription: frequency, intensity (and how it
  was anchored — %HRmax, %VO₂max, RPE, power zones are not
  interchangeable), duration, volume, progression, and actual completed
  training versus prescribed.
- **The control condition** must be described. Training studies without an
  active control confound the intervention with any structured training.
- Detraining and washout in crossover designs need justification;
  physiological adaptations persist, so carryover is a real threat.
- Athletes' concurrent training and competition schedule are confounders in
  applied studies; report them.

## Athlete populations

- "Trained" and "elite" are used loosely. State the participants' actual
  performance level with objective markers (VO₂max, competitive standard,
  training history) — findings in recreationally active students often
  don't transfer to elite athletes, whose adaptation headroom is much
  smaller.
- Sex differences are substantial in this field and female athletes are
  historically under-studied. Report sex, and where relevant, menstrual
  cycle phase or hormonal contraceptive use with the method used to
  determine it — self-reported phase is unreliable.
- Case studies and case series of individual elite athletes are legitimate
  and valuable, but should be labeled as such rather than framed as
  generalizable findings.

## Ethics and applied constraints

- Maximal exercise testing carries cardiac risk; screening and medical
  supervision should be stated.
- Athletes recruited through their team or coach face implicit pressure —
  the coach-researcher relationship raises the same coercion concern as
  other institutional dual roles.
- Performance-enhancing intervention research has anti-doping implications;
  supplement studies should address purity and contamination testing.
- Commercial sponsorship by supplement and equipment manufacturers is
  common and requires disclosure of sponsor role.

## Publication norms

- Journals: MSSE, Journal of Applied Physiology, IJSPP, Sports Medicine,
  BJSM, Journal of Sports Sciences.
- SportRxiv preprints accepted and growing.
- Registered Reports available at several venues and well suited to the
  small-N problem.
- Data and analysis code sharing is increasing but not yet standard.

## Red flags

- Magnitude-based inference used as the primary inferential method
- Performance changes reported smaller than the measure's typical error
- "Responders vs. non-responders" from a single pre-post comparison
- Training intensity prescribed by one anchor and compared to studies using
  another
- Elite claims from recreationally trained participants
