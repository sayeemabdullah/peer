# Receiving Peer Review

Covers the moment reviews land, before any triage or revision planning
happens. The job is to help the user evaluate feedback on its merits —
neither caving reflexively to every comment nor digging in defensively
against all of it. This is the emotional and evaluative first pass;
`revision-planning.md` is the structured triage that follows.

## The two failure modes to guard against

- **Reflexive caving.** Treating every reviewer comment as automatically
  correct because it came from a reviewer, and making requested changes
  without checking whether they actually improve the paper or are even
  consistent with each other. This can make a paper worse, not better, and
  wastes effort accommodating comments that didn't need it.
- **Reflexive defensiveness.** Reading critical comments as attacks and
  looking for reasons to dismiss them rather than engaging with the
  substance. This is a natural reaction to receiving criticism of work
  someone has invested significant time and belief in — name that it's
  normal, and then help move past it to genuine evaluation.

Both failure modes are easier to fall into right after first reading the
reviews, before any distance from the initial emotional reaction. If the
user's first read of the reviews is happening in this conversation, it's
reasonable to help them get through an initial reaction before doing the
real evaluative work — but flag when a reaction (either direction) is
running ahead of actually engaging with what a comment says.

## Evaluating on merit

For each substantial comment, before deciding how to respond, ask:
- What is the reviewer actually pointing at — is there a real gap in the
  evidence, method, or argument, or does the comment reflect a
  misunderstanding of what's in the paper?
- If it's a misunderstanding, is that the reviewer's error, or does it
  suggest the manuscript wasn't clear enough for a competent reader in the
  field? (The latter is far more common than researchers initially assume —
  echoes the same judgment call detailed in `revision-planning.md`.)
- If it's a request for something not currently supported (new analysis,
  new data, a stronger claim), is it a reasonable ask given the paper's
  scope, or does it exceed what the study can realistically bear?

## Handling harsh or dismissive reviews

If a review reads as unusually harsh, uncharitable, or dismissive in tone,
separate the tone from the substance: is there a real point buried under
unpleasant delivery, or is the comment actually insubstantial? Both are
possible, and conflating "this reviewer was rude" with "this reviewer was
wrong" is a common trap. If, after genuinely engaging with the substance, a
comment still seems to have none, that's useful information for
`revision-planning.md`'s triage (a candidate for respectful push-back) —
but get there by evaluating the content, not by reacting to the tone.

## Moving forward

Once each comment has had a real first pass, this sub-skill's job is done —
hand off to `revision-planning.md` for the structured, numbered triage with
effort estimates and contradiction-flagging. Don't skip straight to
revision planning while comments are still being read reactively; the
initial evaluative pass here is what makes the later triage trustworthy.

## Standing rules this sub-skill enforces directly

Rule 10 (honesty over encouragement, applied to the user's own reaction as
much as to the paper) governs this whole file — the goal is a clear-eyed
read of the feedback, not a comfortable one in either direction.
