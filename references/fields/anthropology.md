# Field Layer: Anthropology

Covers sociocultural anthropology, ethnography, archaeology, and biological
anthropology. Read `qualitative-research.md` first for ethnographic rigor
markers — this file covers what's specific to anthropology's fieldwork
relationship, its subfields' distinct evidence, and its particular ethical
history.

## Ethnographic fieldwork

- **Duration and depth are the method.** Report how long fieldwork lasted,
  its distribution over time, the researcher's language competence, and
  reliance on interpreters. A few weeks of interviews is not ethnography,
  and calling it that overstates the evidence.
- Positionality is substantive, not ritual: how the researcher was
  positioned in the community (kinship, patronage, suspicion, institutional
  affiliation) shaped what could be observed and what people said.
- Distinguish what was observed from what was reported to the researcher
  from what the researcher inferred. These are different evidentiary
  statuses and get conflated in prose.
- Fieldnotes, interviews, and recordings each support different claims;
  say which grounds a given assertion.
- Generalization: ethnographic claims are about a place, a time, and a set
  of relationships. Extending them to "the X people" flattens internal
  variation and is a long-criticized move.

## Archaeology

- **Context is evidence.** Provenience, stratigraphy, and association are
  what make a find interpretable; artifacts without context support far
  weaker claims. Report excavation method, screen size, and sampling
  strategy — recovery method determines what is found.
- **Dating**: report the method, the material dated, laboratory code,
  uncalibrated and calibrated dates with the calibration curve used, and
  uncertainty. Old wood, reservoir effects, and residuality mean a date on a
  sample is not necessarily the date of the event.
- Absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence given preservation bias —
  organic materials, small sites, and perishable technologies are
  systematically under-represented.
- Destructive analysis needs justification and, where relevant, descendant
  community consent.
- **Looted and unprovenanced material**: publishing unprovenanced artifacts
  can increase their market value and incentivize looting. Most journals
  now restrict it; address provenance and legal export explicitly.

## Biological anthropology

- Skeletal and genetic work on human remains carries specific obligations —
  see `genomics-bioinformatics.md` for ancient DNA multiple-testing and
  contamination concerns, plus authenticity criteria for degraded samples.
- Sample size in skeletal populations is limited and often biased by
  preservation and burial practice; the excavated sample is not the living
  population.
- Race is not a valid biological category for structuring analysis; ancestry
  and geographic origin require careful, explicit definition. This field's
  history makes imprecision here consequential.
- **NAGPRA** (US) and equivalent repatriation frameworks govern work with
  Indigenous remains and funerary objects; descendant community consultation
  is a legal and ethical requirement, not a courtesy.

## Ethics and the discipline's history

Anthropology's ethical framework is shaped by its colonial history and by
documented cases of research harming the communities studied.
- **Do no harm** is the primary obligation in the AAA ethics code, and it
  can override the interest in publishing.
- Informed consent is ongoing and often oral; power differentials in
  fieldwork settings make it fragile. Community consultation may be expected
  alongside individual consent.
- Anonymization of people and places is standard practice but conflicts with
  transparency and with communities who want to be credited. It also fails
  easily — a pseudonymous village is often identifiable from context.
  Reason about the tradeoff explicitly.
- Indigenous data sovereignty (CARE principles) and community protocols
  apply; consultation may constrain what can be published or archived.
- Research in conflict zones or with undocumented, criminalized, or
  persecuted populations requires data security planning — subpoena risk
  and seizure are real.

## Publication norms

- Journals: American Anthropologist, Current Anthropology, American
  Ethnologist, Cultural Anthropology, Journal of Archaeological Science,
  American Journal of Biological Anthropology.
- **Monographs carry more weight than in most fields** and are often the
  primary form of ethnographic publication — a consideration for
  `submission-strategy.md`.
- Current Anthropology publishes commentaries alongside articles.
- Data sharing is genuinely constrained by confidentiality and community
  agreements; say what can and cannot be shared and why, rather than
  treating non-sharing as a deficiency.

## Red flags

- Short-term interviewing presented as ethnography
- Claims generalized from one community to a named ethnic group
- Archaeological interpretation from artifacts without provenience
- Unprovenanced material published with no provenance discussion
- Work with Indigenous communities or remains with no consultation
  described
